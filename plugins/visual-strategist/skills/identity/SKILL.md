# Visual identity

A visual identity is the set of signs people learn to recognise a project by, and the rules that keep those signs consistent wherever it appears: the app and its store listing, the website, social posts, video, slides, documents, print, packaging, merchandise, events and streams. The interface is one touchpoint among many. Build the identity before the interface, or alongside it, so the interface inherits it instead of inventing its own.

## Background knowledge

Use these ideas to explain your choices, and name each one the first time you use it.

### How images make meaning

- **Semiotics.** A sign pairs a form (the signifier) with a meaning (the signified). Peirce named three kinds. An icon resembles its object, like a camera glyph. An index points to its object by cause or contact, like a footprint or a "typing…" indicator. A symbol means something by convention only, like a heart for "like". Symbols are the cheapest to draw and the most expensive to teach.
- **Denotation and connotation** (Barthes). Denotation is what an image literally shows; connotation is what it suggests. A green tick denotes "done", and in a finance app it also connotes "safe". Identity work lives in connotation. When text sits next to an image, it either fixes which meaning to read (anchorage) or adds what the image can't say (relay).
- **Iconography and iconology** (Panofsky). Read an image in three passes. First, describe what is shown. Second, identify the conventional subject and its symbols. Third, interpret what the choices reveal about the values of whoever made it, and who it was made for. Critique references in this order.
- **Attributes and motifs.** An attribute is an object that identifies a figure, like a saint's key or a detective's pipe. A motif is an element that recurs. Give a project one or two of each, and repeat them until people link them to it.
- **Visual rhetoric.** Every visual makes an argument. It persuades through ethos (credibility: craft, restraint, consistency), pathos (emotion: colour, light, faces, motion) and logos (clarity: hierarchy, evidence, data). Its levers are composition, colour and light, framing and camera angle, typography, and association. The figures of speech work in images too: metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, juxtaposition, hyperbole and repetition.
- **Category codes.** Every category has conventions people use to recognise it, like blue and shields for security, or pastels and rounded type for children. Use enough of them that people understand what the project is, and break the rest on purpose.

### How people see

- **Gestalt.** People group what they see by proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, common fate and figure and ground, and they settle on the simplest reading (Prägnanz). A mark that fights these principles reads as noise.
- **Pre-attentive features.** Hue, size, orientation, motion and contrast register before anyone reads. Spend them on what matters most, one at a time per view.
- **Hierarchy.** Differences in size, weight, value and space set the reading order. If everything is emphasised, nothing is.
- **Colour is relative** (Albers). The same swatch looks different on different grounds, at different sizes, and on different screens and papers. Judge colour in context, never as a chip on its own. Build scales in a perceptual space such as OKLCH, so that each step looks even.
- **Colour meaning is cultural.** White, red, yellow and green mean different things across cultures, industries and moments. Check them against the markets in the brief, and never let colour carry a meaning alone.

### What makes an asset work

- **Fame and uniqueness** (Romaniuk). A brand asset works when people link it to the project (fame) and to no one else (uniqueness). New assets have neither. They earn both through consistent use over years, so changing assets often throws that work away.
- **Distinctive beats different.** People rarely remember how a brand differs, but they remember how it looks. Aim for assets that people recognise at a glance, from a fragment, at a small size and in one colour.
- **Fixed or fluid.** A fixed identity repeats the same signs exactly. A fluid identity keeps a few constants, such as a shape, a typeface or a colour logic, and lets everything else vary. Fluid systems need stronger constants and clearer rules, not fewer.
- **Brand architecture.** When there are several products, decide early whether they share one master brand, carry endorsed sub-brands or stand as separate brands. That choice decides what every product shares and what each one may own.

### The elements of an identity

For each element, know its job, its forms and how it fails.

- **Name and verbal identity:** the name, tagline and tone of voice. They aren't yours to define, but ask for them, because they constrain the visuals.
- **Logo.** The kinds are wordmark, lettermark, pictorial mark, abstract mark, emblem, mascot, combination mark and dynamic mark. It needs a set of lock-ups (horizontal, stacked, symbol only), a clear space, a minimum size, one-colour and reversed versions, and a favicon or app icon that still reads at 16 px.
- **Colour.** Assign roles before hues: brand, surface, text, accent and feedback. Pick one ownable primary, a neutral range that does most of the work, and few accents. Specify values for screen (hex, OKLCH) and for print (CMYK, plus a spot colour where it matters).
- **Typography.** Use one or two families with clear roles, such as display, text and data. Check the licence covers app, web and print, the glyphs cover every language in the brief, and the type still works at small sizes.
- **Iconography.** Define a grid, keyline shapes, stroke weight, corners, terminals, fill rules and a library of metaphors. Icons should look drawn by the same hand as the logo and type.
- **Illustration and characters.** Set rules for line, shape language, palette, texture and perspective, and for what may and may not be drawn. A mascot needs a turnaround, expressions, poses and a list of things it never does.
- **Imagery and art direction.** Set the subjects, framing, light, colour grade and casting, and what never appears. Add treatments that make stock and user photos look like the project's own.
- **Graphic devices.** Patterns, shapes, frames, textures and crops taken from the mark. They carry the identity where the logo is absent.
- **Layout.** Margins, columns, a spacing scale and composition habits that hold from a phone screen to a poster.
- **Motion.** Timing, easing, anticipation and follow-through, a few signature behaviours, and a logo animation. Motion is part of the identity, not decoration on top of it.
- **Data, sound and video.** Charts follow the colour and type rules. The sonic logo and video belong to whoever owns sound and video, such as a sound or video strategist, so flag where timing and character must agree with theirs.

### Touchpoints beyond the interface

List the project's real touchpoints in the brief, and test the identity on the hardest ones: the app icon and store listing, website, social avatars and posts, video thumbnails and title cards, slides, documents and email, print (cards, posters, packaging, labels), merchandise, signage and events, streams and overlays, and in-product or in-game assets. An identity that only works inside its own interface isn't finished.

## Process

1. **Foundations.** Run the method's discovery, and also ask: why the project exists, what it competes with for attention, its personality in three words and the opposite of each, its name and voice, every touchpoint, how long the identity must last, and whether more products will share it.
2. **Audit.** Collect what exists, both the project's own work and its category's. Map the category codes. Note which existing assets are worth keeping because people already know them.
3. **Territories.** Before drawing any logo, propose two or three conceptual territories. Each one gets an idea, a moodboard and a few sketches, and says what it would make people believe.
4. **Core assets.** Within the chosen territory, develop the mark, colour, type and one graphic device. Test each one small, in one colour, on light and dark, as a fragment, and next to the main competitors.
5. **System.** Extend into iconography, illustration, imagery, layout and motion. Every element must trace back to the core idea.
6. **Application tests.** Mock up the real touchpoints from the brief, including the awkward ones. When a mock-up breaks, fix the system, not just the mock-up.
7. **Guidelines.** Write the identity spec: the idea, each asset and its files, rules with correct and incorrect examples, and how to design a touchpoint nobody has made yet. Record each decision in the brief.

## Standards

- Start from meaning. Say what each element should make people believe before you draw it.
- Test recognition at 16 px, in one colour, at a glance and from a fragment.
- Prefer a few strong assets used consistently over many weak ones.
- Look for similar marks in the category, and say that a professional trademark search is needed before launch. Don't give legal advice.
- Deliver files that work in production: SVG and PDF masters, PNG exports at the sizes each touchpoint needs, colour values for screen and print, and a list of font licences.
